Getting Started

Turkish for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Turkish is spoken by more than 80 million people in Turkey and by large communities in Germany, the Netherlands, France and beyond. Learners often arrive expecting difficulty and leave surprised: Turkish is one of the most logical languages in the world. Its spelling is almost perfectly honest — you pronounce what you see — and its grammar runs on regular rules with almost no exceptions.

A language built like Lego

Turkish builds words by stacking suffixes onto a root, a process called agglutination. From ev (house) you get evim (my house), evimde (in my house) and even evimdekiler (the ones in my house). Each piece keeps its shape and its meaning, so long words stop being scary once you learn to read them piece by piece, from left to right.

How Turkish sentences are built

English puts the verb in the middle: "I drink tea." Turkish puts it at the end: Ben çay içerim (ben chay icherim — literally "I tea drink"). Once your ear accepts subject–object–verb order, Turkish sentences become predictable. There are no articles like "a" or "the", no grammatical gender, and verbs follow tidy patterns instead of long lists of irregular forms.

The first words to learn

Start with survival words: merhaba (hello), teşekkür ederim (thank you), su (water), yemek (food), ne kadar? (how much?) and nerede? (where?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already handle taxis, tea houses and bazaar shopping. Our everyday vocabulary guide gives you sixty of the most useful words in one place.

Spelling you can trust

Turkish adopted the Latin alphabet in 1928, and the fit is excellent: each letter has one sound, and each sound has one letter. The few special letters — ç, ğ, ı, İ, ö, ş, ü — are easy once someone explains them, which our alphabet and vowel harmony guide does in full. If you can read a Turkish word aloud, you can spell it, and vice versa.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on sounds and spelling, five on new words, and five speaking aloud — even to yourself. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your job, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones.

Above all, speak from the first week. A single merhaba in an Istanbul tea house will teach you more about Turkish hospitality than a chapter of grammar. Teşekkürler — and welcome to the language.

The Alphabet

The Turkish Alphabet and Vowel Harmony

Turkish spelling is the friendliest in Europe: one letter, one sound, almost without exception. The alphabet has 29 letters — the Latin alphabet minus q, w and x, plus six special letters of its own. Learn these, and you can read any Turkish sign, menu or message aloud with confidence.

The special letters

Most letters sound as an English speaker would expect. The ones to watch are: c sounds like the "j" in "jam" (cam — glass, said "jam"); ç is "ch" (çay — tea); ş is "sh" (şehir — city); j is the soft French "zh" sound in "measure". The vowels ö and ü are rounded sounds borrowed from German-style pronunciation: say "ee" with rounded lips for ü, and "eh" with rounded lips for ö.

The two i's: ı and İ

Turkish famously has two i letters. The dotless ı is a deep sound from the back of the throat, close to the "a" in "about" — it appears in kız (girl) and ışık (light). The dotted capital İ pairs with small i and sounds like the "ee" in "see". This is why the city is written İstanbul with a dotted capital İ — a detail Turkish keyboards take very seriously.

The silent ğ

The letter ğ (yumuşak g, "soft g") never starts a word and is never pronounced as a hard g. It either lengthens the vowel before it — dağ (mountain) is said "daa" — or melts into a faint "y" glide between vowels, as in öğretmen (teacher). Once you stop trying to pronounce it, Turkish reading becomes much easier.

Vowel harmony: the golden rule

Turkish vowels divide into two families: front vowels (e, i, ö, ü), made at the front of the mouth, and back vowels (a, ı, o, u), made at the back. Suffixes must harmonise with the last vowel of the word they attach to. The plural of ev (house, front vowel) is evler, but the plural of araba (car, back vowel) is arabalar. The suffix vowel follows the word like a shadow.

Why this matters to you

Vowel harmony is why Turkish sounds so musical, and it is also a gift to learners: if you know a word's last vowel, you can predict the shape of almost every suffix it will take. Our sentence-building guide shows harmony at work inside real suffix chains.

Practise by reading shop signs and street names aloud, and check any word that puzzles you in the translator. Within a few weeks, Turkish text stops being a puzzle and starts being a pleasure.

Better Results

How to Translate English to Turkish Accurately

Machine translation between English and Turkish has improved enormously, but the two languages are built very differently, so small habits on your side make a big difference to the result. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often.

1. Write complete sentences

Fragments confuse translators because Turkish verb endings carry information English leaves to context. "Going tomorrow" could be a question, a plan or a command. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Turkish comes out clean: Yarın gidiyorum (yarin gidiyorum).

2. Keep sentences short

Turkish puts the verb last, so a forty-word English sentence becomes a long walk to the verb. Split long sentences into two or three short ones before translating; the output will be dramatically more natural.

3. Use punctuation properly

Commas and full stops tell the translator where ideas end. A missing full stop can glue two thoughts into one strange Turkish sentence. Question marks matter too — Turkish questions use a separate question particle (mı, mi, mu, mü), and the translator places it correctly when it can see your "?" clearly.

4. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces very confused Turkish. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

5. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words. If a person's or company's name must stay in English letters, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards. Watch Turkish place names too: the official English exonyms (Istanbul, Ankara) differ from the Turkish spellings (İstanbul) only slightly, but the translator handles both.

6. Choose the right level of politeness

Turkish has two words for "you": friendly sen and polite siz, and verbs change to match. For elders, officials and anyone you have just met, add words like "sir", "madam" or "please" to your English — translators usually respond by choosing the respectful siz forms.

7. Watch out for English words with several meanings

"Book" the noun (kitap) and "book" the verb (rezervasyon yapmak) are different Turkish expressions. If the result looks odd, add a clarifying word in English — "book a table", "read a book" — and translate again.

8. Translate paragraph by paragraph

Long pasted documents drift. Work one paragraph at a time, and keep a note of names and technical terms so they stay consistent from start to finish.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Turkish result back into the translator in the Turkish-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a Turkey trip.

Travel

Essential Turkish Phrases for Travelling in Turkey

Turkey rewards travellers who try even a little Turkish. English is common in Istanbul's tourist districts and the coastal resorts, but a greeting in Turkish at a tea house, a dolmuş stop or a village bakery changes the whole conversation. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Start every interaction with merhaba (hello). In the morning say günaydın (good morning), and when you sit down anywhere, expect the warm reply hoş geldiniz (welcome) — answer hoş bulduk (glad to be here) if you want to delight your host. Thank everyone with teşekkür ederim; it is always noticed.

Getting around

For taxis and dolmuş minibuses: ne kadar? (how much?) settles the fare. Lütfen burada durun (please stop here), sola dönün (turn left) and sağa dönün (turn right) handle directions, and yavaş lütfen (slowly, please) is worth remembering on mountain roads. For Istanbul's ferries and metro, istasyon nerede? (where is the station?) points you the right way.

Food and drink

Turkey runs on tea and legendary breakfasts. Ask bir çay lütfen (one tea, please), declare vejetaryenim (I am vegetarian) if needed, and praise the food with çok lezzetli (very delicious). When you finish a meal, your host may say afiyet olsun — the Turkish "bon appétit", used both before and after eating.

Shopping and bargaining

Bu ne kadar? (how much is this?) opens every negotiation. In bazaars, gentle bargaining is part of the culture; smile and ask indirim yapar mısınız? (can you give a discount?). Finish with tamam, alıyorum (okay, I'll take it) when you are happy — and expect a glass of tea to appear while you decide.

At the hotel

Check-in runs on a handful of phrases: rezervasyonum var (I have a reservation), boş odanız var mı? (do you have a free room?), kahvaltı dahil mi? (is breakfast included?) and anahtarı alabilir miyim? (could I have the key?). If something is wrong, Turks respond best to a smile plus küçük bir sorun var (there is a small problem) — understatement opens doors, in hotels as in bazaars.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: yardım edin! (help!), doktor çağırın (call a doctor) and hastane nerede? (where is the hospital?). Turkey's single emergency number is 112. People in Turkey are famously quick to help a traveller in trouble — often before you finish the sentence.

Numbers & Counting

Turkish Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In Turkey you will use them for taxi fares, bazaar prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and train times — often several times a day. The good news is that Turkish counting is beautifully regular: learn twenty words and you can say almost any number.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: bir (one), iki (two), üç (three), dört (four), beş (five), altı (six), yedi (seven), sekiz (eight), dokuz (nine) and on (ten). Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The tens: 20 to 100

Each ten has its own name: yirmi (twenty), otuz (thirty), kırk (forty), elli (fifty), altmış (sixty), yetmiş (seventy), seksen (eighty), doksan (ninety) and yüz (one hundred). Numbers in between are simple addition: twenty-five is yirmi beş, and ninety-nine is doksan dokuz. No hyphens, no tricks.

Hundreds, thousands and millions

For bigger numbers: yüz (hundred), bin (thousand) and milyon (million). Two hundred is iki yüz and five thousand is beş bin. One special case: a single hundred or thousand is just yüz or bin — Turkish does not add "bir" in front the way English adds "one".

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: bu ne kadar? (how much is this?). Zero is sıfır, which you will need for phone numbers, given digit by digit. For quantities, put the number first and keep the noun singular: iki çay (two teas) — Turkish never pluralises after a number, which is one less rule to remember.

First, second, third — and halves

Ordering numbers work differently: first is birinci, second is ikinci, third is üçüncü, and from fourth onwards the pattern settles into the number plus -inci/-ıncı: dördüncü, beşinci. You will use them for floors, stops and platforms — ikinci kat is the second floor. Two fractions matter daily: yarım (half — also used for half past the hour) and çeyrek (quarter).

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, glasses of tea — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make bazaar day much easier.

How to make these words stick

Do not try to memorise all seventy words in one sitting. Take one theme a day, say each word aloud, and immediately build a tiny sentence around it — su istiyorum (I want water), bu benim evim (this is my house). Within two weeks the house itself becomes your Turkish teacher.

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Turkish

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a ferry, fixing a meeting or planning around the summer heat, you need days, dates and clock times. Turkish keeps this simple: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make.

The seven days

The Turkish week starts on Monday: Pazartesi (Monday), Salı (Tuesday), Çarşamba (Wednesday), Perşembe (Thursday), Cuma (Friday), Cumartesi (Saturday) and Pazar (Sunday). Two are easy to remember: Pazartesi is literally "the day after Pazar", and Cumartesi is "the day after Cuma".

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: bugün (today), yarın (tomorrow) and dün (yesterday). "See you tomorrow" is yarın görüşürüz — a phrase worth using daily. Add her gün (every day) and hafta (week) and you can talk about almost any schedule.

Parts of the day

The day divides into sabah (morning), öğle (midday, noon), akşam (evening) and gece (night). Greetings follow the same pattern — günaydın for good morning, iyi akşamlar for good evening and iyi geceler for good night.

Months of the year

The twelve months are: Ocak (January), Şubat (February), Mart (March), Nisan (April), Mayıs (May), Haziran (June), Temmuz (July), Ağustos (August), Eylül (September), Ekim (October), Kasım (November) and Aralık (December). Dates use the day first, then the month, then the year — 24 Kasım 2026.

Asking and telling the time

Ask saat kaç? (what time is it? — literally "the hour is how much?"). The word saat means both "hour" and "clock". Answers are simple on the hour: saat beş (it is five o'clock). Half past uses buçuk: beş buçuk is half past five. A quarter is çeyrek — beşi çeyrek geçiyor is quarter past five — and minutes before the hour use a different ending, altıya on var (ten to six). Start with the on-the-hour and buçuk forms; locals will happily fill in the rest.

Seasons and relative time

The four seasons are ilkbahar (spring), yaz (summer), sonbahar (autumn) and kış (winter). For planning further out, Turkish swaps the word order English uses: next week is gelecek hafta ("the coming week") and last week is geçen hafta ("the passing week"); the same pattern gives gelecek yıl (next year) and geçen ay (last month). For appointments, randevu is the word — yarın saat üçte randevum var means "I have an appointment tomorrow at three".

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the country. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family

Family Words in Turkish: Relatives and Relationships

Family sits at the centre of life in Turkey, and Turkish has a warm, precise word for almost every relationship — including the age order that English ignores. Learning these words does more than build vocabulary: it explains why half the people in a Turkish street seem to be someone's "abi" or "abla" (elder brother, elder sister), related or not.

Parents and grandparents

Father is baba and mother is anne — two of the most loved words in the language. Grandfather is dede and grandmother is nine (you will also hear babaanne for a paternal grandmother and anneanne for a maternal one). The general word for family itself is aile.

Brothers and sisters

Turkish always asks: older or younger? An elder brother is ağabey — usually shortened to "abi" in speech — and an elder sister is abla. These words double as respectful titles for any slightly older man or woman: waiters, drivers and shopkeepers are all abi and abla. A younger brother is erkek kardeş and a younger sister is kız kardeş; the neutral word kardeş covers any sibling younger than you or when age does not matter.

Uncles and aunts

Here Turkish is more detailed than English. Your father's brother is amca, your mother's brother is dayı, and your father's sister is hala while your mother's sister is teyze. All four words are also everyday polite titles — an older taxi driver is "amca", the lady at the market is "teyze" — and using them correctly earns instant warmth. Husbands of aunts are enişte, another word you will hear constantly.

Children and the next generation

A son is oğul (often oğlum, "my son", used affectionately even to strangers' children) and a daughter is kız. A child is çocuk and children are çocuklar. A husband is koca, a wife is karı in older usage, but the polite modern word for either spouse is eş. A grandchild is torun.

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is bu benim annem. "I have two sisters" is iki kız kardeşim var — notice var (there is / I have), one of the most useful words in Turkish. Both frames return in our self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in Turkey, not prying — asking about someone's aile shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in the country.

Speaking

Turkish Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in Turkey begins and ends with warmth, and Turkish has a specialised greeting for almost every situation — including two famous phrases English does not have at all. Master this small set and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again: in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

The all-purpose merhaba

Merhaba works at any hour, for any age, in any setting — hello and respectful greeting in one word. When someone greets you first, simply return the same word with a smile. The warmer selam is the casual "hi" between friends and younger people. When you arrive anywhere as a guest, you will be greeted with hoş geldiniz (welcome); the gracious reply is hoş bulduk — literally "we found it pleasant".

Greetings by time of day

Turkish also has time-specific greetings: günaydın (good morning), iyi günler (good day — also used when leaving a shop in the daytime), iyi akşamlar (good evening) and iyi geceler (good night). In casual daily life, plain merhaba is more common than all of them.

Kolay gelsin: the greeting for work

Turkish has a phrase English speakers always wish they had: kolay gelsin — "may it come easily". Say it to anyone who is working: the baker, the bus driver, the colleague at a desk, the neighbour sweeping the steps. It is the correct, kind thing to say, and Turks say it dozens of times a day. You will hear it constantly once you start listening for it.

At the table and for the sick

Before, during and after a meal, the phrase is afiyet olsun — "may it be good for you", the Turkish bon appétit. When someone is ill, has had an accident, or has come through anything unpleasant, the words are geçmiş olsun — "may it be in the past", the standard get-well wish. And when someone has bought something new, from shoes to a car, congratulate them with hayırlı olsun (may it bring good) or güle güle kullanın (use it with smiles).

Asking how someone is

The standard question is nasılsınız? (how are you? — polite), or nasılsın? between friends. The usual answer is iyiyim, teşekkür ederim (I am fine, thank you), often followed by siz nasılsınız? (and how are you?). Among close friends you will hear the cheerful ne haber? — "what's the news?", Turkey's version of "what's up?".

Saying goodbye — Turkish style

Goodbyes split by who is moving. The person leaving says hoşça kalın ("stay pleasantly"); the person staying replies güle güle ("go with smiles"). Between friends, görüşürüz (see you) is the natural everyday goodbye, and kendinize iyi bakın (take care of yourself) is the warm way to end a longer parting.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Turkish: Bazaar Phrases That Work

Turkey's markets — the Grand Bazaar's vaulted lanes, the spice pyramids of the Egyptian Bazaar, the weekly neighbourhood pazar with its shouting vendors — run on friendly talk. In traditional markets, prices are often a conversation, not a label. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with merhaba or iyi günler. If you are just browsing, say sadece bakıyorum (I am just looking). To ask for something specific: bunun başka rengi var mı? (do you have this in another colour?). The word for shop is dükkan and for market, pazar — which is also the word for Sunday, the traditional market day.

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: bu ne kadar? (how much is this?). For things sold by weight, ask kilosu ne kadar? (how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, tekrar söyler misiniz? (could you say it again?) buys you a slower repeat — and probably a calculator held up helpfully.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: daha büyük bedeni var mı? (do you have a bigger size?) and daha küçük beden (smaller size). Common colours: kırmızı (red), mavi (blue), yeşil (green), siyah (black), beyaz (white) and sarı (yellow).

Bargaining, politely — the art of pazarlık

In bazaars (less so in fixed-price shops and malls), bargaining — pazarlık — is expected and even enjoyed. Open with biraz pahalı (a little expensive), then ask indirim yapar mısınız? (can you do a discount?) or offer your price with şu fiyata olur mu? (would this price work?). Tea will likely appear; accepting it does not oblige you to buy. Accept a deal with tamam, anlaştık (okay, we have a deal), or decline kindly with teşekkürler, düşünmem lazım (thank you, I need to think).

Paying and finishing

Ask for the bill with hesabı alabilir miyim?. Cash is nakit; the change you receive back is para üstü. Cards are accepted almost everywhere in cities — kredi kartı geçerli mi? (do you take credit cards?) settles it in advance.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Turkish, even imperfect Turkish, changes the whole shopping experience — and usually the price.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Turkish

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. Turkey has modern hospitals, pharmacies on every main street, and people who rush to help strangers. These phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The words that cut through noise are yardım edin! (help!) and imdat! (help! — a stronger cry for urgent danger). To stop someone urgently: durun! (stop!). Turkey's single emergency number is 112 — it covers ambulance, police and fire services nationwide, and operators in major cities often have access to English support. Save it in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: hastane nerede? (where is the hospital?), doktor çağırın (please call a doctor), and ambulans çağırın (call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is doktor and for a hospital, hastane. State hospitals are devlet hastanesi and private ones özel hastane.

Describing what is wrong

Start with kendimi iyi hissetmiyorum (I am not well). The word ağrı (pain, ache) combines with body parts: baş ağrısı (headache), karın ağrısı (stomach ache), diş ağrısı (toothache). Other common words: ateş (fever), öksürük (cough), kan (blood) and alerji (allergy). "It hurts here" is burada ağrıyor — point as you say it. If you take regular medication, bu ilacı kullanıyorum (I use this medicine) plus showing the packet works wonders.

At the pharmacy

A pharmacy is an eczane — look for the red "E" sign. One pharmacy in every neighbourhood stays open all night as the nöbetçi eczane (duty pharmacy); its name is posted in every pharmacy window. Medicine is ilaç. Useful lines: bu ilaç var mı? (do you have this medicine?) and günde kaç kez kullanmalıyım? (how many times a day should I take it?). Turkish pharmacists are highly trained and often the fastest route to sensible advice for minor problems.

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: polisi arayın (call the police). "I have lost my bag" is çantamı kaybettim, and "I don't understand" — anlamıyorum — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed. In tourist areas you may also meet the turizm polisi (tourism police), who are used to helping visitors.

Grammar Basics

Turkish Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Questions are the engine of conversation. With a handful of Turkish question words and the personal pronouns, you can ask for directions, prices, names, reasons and recommendations — and understand the answers when they come back. This guide gives you the full toolkit in one place.

The question words

Learn these eight and you can ask almost anything: ne (what), kim (who), nerede (where), ne zaman (when), neden or niçin (why), nasıl (how), kaç (how many, how much — for countable things) and hangi (which). Where-questions extend naturally: nereden (from where) and nereye (to where) are the same word wearing different suffixes — a pattern our sentence guide explains.

Building your first questions

Question words usually sit just before the verb, where the answer will land. Bu ne? (what is this?), Tuvalet nerede? (where is the toilet?), Tren ne zaman geliyor? (when is the train coming?), Nasıl gidilir? (how does one get there?) and the traveller's favourite, Bu ne kadar? (how much is this?) — kaç questions take a number as their answer, which is why the numbers guide pairs so well with this one.

The personal pronouns

Turkish pronouns are short and regular: ben (I), sen (you — friendly), o (he, she or it — one word for all three), biz (we), siz (you — polite or plural) and onlar (they). Because verb endings already show who is acting, pronouns are often dropped entirely: geliyorum on its own already means "I am coming". You will add ben and sen mainly for emphasis or contrast.

The question particle: mı, mi, mu, mü

Yes-or-no questions in Turkish use a small separate word that follows the word being questioned: Geliyor musunuz? (are you coming?), Türkçe biliyor musunuz? (do you know Turkish?). The particle changes shape by vowel harmony — mı after a/ı, mi after e/i, mu after o/u, mü after ö/ü — and it is always written separately, a spelling rule Turks are strict about. Answer with evet (yes) or hayır (no), and you are in a real conversation.

Sen or siz? Choosing your "you"

Use siz with strangers, elders, officials and in shops and offices; use sen with friends, children and people your own age who offer it. When in doubt, start with siz — nobody is offended by extra politeness, and people will quickly invite you to switch if the setting is informal. Culture

Turkish Festivals and Culture: Words and Wishes

Turkey's calendar mixes religious festivals, national days and joyful seasonal traditions, and each one comes with its own greetings. Sending the right wish at the right moment — even in simple Turkish — is one of the fastest ways to make friends in Turkey. Here are the celebrations that matter most, and exactly what to say.

Ramadan: the holy month

During Ramazan (Ramadan), many Turks fast from dawn to sunset. The evening meal that breaks the fast is iftar, and the pre-dawn meal is sahur. A thoughtful wish for the month is hayırlı Ramazanlar (may Ramadan be blessed for you). If you are invited to an iftar, arrive hungry, accept everything graciously, and say afiyet olsun as the food arrives.

Ramazan Bayramı: the Sugar Festival

The three-day festival ending Ramadan is called Ramazan Bayramı — and, for its sweets, Şeker Bayramı (the Sugar Festival). Families visit elders, children kiss grandparents' hands and receive sweets and small gifts of money, and every home offers baklava and Turkish delight. The greeting is iyi bayramlar (happy festival) or bayramınız mübarek olsun (may your festival be blessed).

Kurban Bayramı: the Feast of Sacrifice

About two months later comes the four-day Kurban Bayramı, honouring Abraham's willingness to sacrifice, marked by charity, family visits and shared meals — much of the meat is traditionally given to those in need. The same greetings apply: iyi bayramlar works for both festivals, and bayramınız kutlu olsun (may your festival be celebrated/happy) is equally common.

Republic Day and national days

On 29 October, Cumhuriyet Bayramı (Republic Day) celebrates the founding of the Republic of Turkey in 1923: flags appear on every building, and the wish is Cumhuriyet Bayramımız kutlu olsun. Other national days include 23 April (National Sovereignty and Children's Day — Turkey famously gives the day to its children) and 19 May (commemorating Atatürk, and Youth and Sports Day).

New Year, birthdays and everyday wishes

New Year is celebrated warmly: yeni yılınız kutlu olsun or mutlu yıllar (happy new year). Birthdays take doğum günün kutlu olsun (happy birthday, friendly) or iyi ki doğdun (glad you were born — a beloved phrase). For weddings, the wish is bir ömür boyu mutluluklar (a lifetime of happiness), and for a new baby, gözünüz aydın (may your eyes shine — congratulations on joyful news).

Speaking

How to Introduce Yourself in Turkish

Introducing yourself is the first real conversation you will ever have in Turkish — at a guesthouse, in a language class, over a new neighbour's tea. This guide builds your introduction piece by piece, so you can say it with confidence and understand the replies that come back.

Saying your name

Start with merhaba and the simplest frame in Turkish: benim adım ... (my name is ...). To ask theirs: sizin adınız ne? (what is your name? — polite) or senin adın ne? between new friends. Follow with the set phrase tanıştığımıza memnun oldum (nice to meet you) — Turks use it at every first meeting, and the expected reply is ben de memnun oldum (I am pleased too).

Where you are from

The pattern is country plus the suffix -lı/-li/-lu/-lü (from): Ben Pakistanlıyım (I am from Pakistan), Ben İngiltereliyim would be wrong — Britain gives İngilizim (I am English/British). Common ones: Amerikalıyım (I am American), Almanım (I am German), Hintliyim (I am Indian). For your city, use ...da yaşıyorum (I live in ...): Londra'da yaşıyorum (I live in London). And if you are visiting: turistim, tatil için geldim (I am a tourist, I came for a holiday).

Age, family and work

Age uses yaşında: otuz yaşındayım (I am thirty years old) — combine it with our numbers. Family: evliyim (I am married), bekarım (I am single), iki çocuğum var (I have two children). Work uses your job title plus -ım/-im: öğretmenim (I am a teacher), mühendisim (I am an engineer), öğrenciyim (I am a student). To ask someone else, the polite question is ne iş yapıyorsunuz? (what work do you do?).

A full worked example

Here is a complete introduction, line by line. Merhaba, benim adım Alex. (Hello, my name is Alex.) İngiltereliyim, Londra'da yaşıyorum. (I am from England, I live in London.) Otuz iki yaşındayım. (I am thirty-two.) Mühendisim. (I am an engineer.) Evliyim ve bir kızım var. (I am married and I have one daughter.) Türkçe öğreniyorum. (I am learning Turkish.) Tanıştığımıza memnun oldum. (Nice to meet you.) Six short lines — and you have had your first real Turkish conversation.

Handling the replies

Expect warmth and follow-up questions about your family — use our family words to answer. If the Turkish gets too fast, lütfen yavaş konuşun (please speak slowly) and Türkçem çok iyi değil (my Turkish is not very good) will earn you patience and encouragement every time.

Food & Drink

Turkish for Food Lovers: Eating Well in Turkey

Turkish cuisine is regularly ranked among the world's great food cultures — and eating in Turkey is half the reason many travellers come. The language of the table is learnable in an afternoon, and this guide gives you the dishes, the drinks and the phrases that turn a good meal into a great one.

Kahvaltı: the famous Turkish breakfast

Breakfast — kahvaltı, literally "before coffee" — is a spread, not a dish: cheeses (peynir), olives (zeytin), tomatoes, cucumbers, eggs, honey with cream (bal kaymak), jams, and the sesame ring bread simit. The star egg dish is menemen — eggs scrambled with tomatoes, peppers and spices. Tea flows endlessly; coffee, despite the name, traditionally comes afterwards.

Kebap and the grill

Kebap covers a family of grilled meats. Know your varieties: Adana kebap (spicy minced lamb, named after its city), Urfa kebap (the same, without the heat), şiş kebap (cubed meat on skewers), döner (meat sliced from the vertical spit) and İskender (döner over bread with tomato sauce, butter and yogurt). Chicken versions swap in tavuk (chicken).

Pide, lahmacun and soups

Pide is the boat-shaped flatbread baked with cheese (kaşarlı), minced meat (kıymalı) or egg. Lahmacun is its thin, crispy cousin spread with spiced mince, rolled up with parsley and a squeeze of lemon. Every meal can start with soup — çorba: mercimek çorbası (red lentil soup) is the beloved classic, served with lemon and dried mint.

Mezes and sweets

Small starter plates are meze: humus, stuffed vine leaves (yaprak sarma), white cheese and melon in summer. For dessert, the icons are baklava (pistachio or walnut, from Gaziantep if you are lucky), künefe (hot cheese pastry in syrup, eaten immediately), sütlaç (baked rice pudding) and lokum — Turkish delight.

Tea and coffee culture

Çay (tea) arrives in tulip-shaped glasses, offered in shops, offices, ferries and bazaars — accepting is polite, refusing needs a smile and a teşekkürler, içmeyeceğim (thanks, I won't have any). Turkish coffee — Türk kahvesi — is ordered by sweetness: sade (no sugar), orta (medium) or şekerli (sweet). An old saying promises that a coffee shared is remembered for forty years.

Useful table phrases

Order with bir ... alabilir miyim? (could I have a ...?). Say acı olsun (make it spicy) or acısız (not spicy), declare vejetaryenim (I am vegetarian), praise with çok lezzetli, ellerinize sağlık (very delicious, health to your hands — the compliment every Turkish cook loves), and finish with hesabı alabilir miyim? (the bill, please).

Take this guide to dinner with the phrasebook open, and check unfamiliar menu words in the translator. In Turkey, a little language at the table is always rewarded. Afiyet olsun!

Grammar Basics

How Turkish Sentences Are Built: Suffixes and Word Order

Turkish grammar has a reputation for long words, but its logic is closer to arithmetic than to the irregular grammar of English. Almost everything Turkish needs to say is done by attaching small, regular suffixes to a root word. Learn how the pieces stack, and the language's longest words become friendly.

Word order: the verb comes last

English order is Subject–Verb–Object: "The student reads the book." Turkish order is Subject–Object–Verb: Öğrenci kitabı okuyor — literally "student book reads". Time and place phrases usually come before the object: Ben yarın İstanbul'a gidiyorum (I — tomorrow — to Istanbul — am going). Once you expect the verb at the end, listening becomes much easier: the action always lands last.

Suffix chains: one word, whole phrase

Watch a root grow. Ev means "house". Add the plural: evler (houses). Add "my": evlerim (my houses). Add "in": evlerimde (in my houses). Add "from" instead: evlerimden (from my houses). Each suffix does exactly one job, in a fixed order — plural, then possession, then case — like carriages coupled behind a locomotive. Turkish even has a famous party-trick word, evimdekiler (the ones in my house), built entirely by this stacking.

Vowel harmony chooses the vowels

Suffix vowels are not fixed; they harmonise with the word's last vowel (the rule from our alphabet guide). "In" is -de after front vowels (evde — in the house) but -da after back vowels (okulda — at school). Two-shape suffixes (a/e) and four-shape ones (ı/i/u/ü) follow the same shadow rule, so once you hear a word, its suffixes are predictable.

Verbs: tense and person in the ending

Turkish verb endings carry both tense and person, which is why pronouns are often dropped. From gelmek (to come): geliyorum (I am coming), geldim (I came), geleceğim (I will come), gelirim (I come, habitually). The person endings are a tidy set — -im for "I", -sin for "you", -iz for "we", -siniz for polite "you" — reused across every tense. Negatives slot in before the tense ending: gelmiyorum (I am not coming).

No gender, no articles, no "is"

Three English headaches simply do not exist in Turkish. Nouns have no gender. There is no word for "a" or "the" — bir (one) can mark "a" when needed. And present-tense "is" is usually just a suffix or nothing at all: O öğretmen means "she/he is a teacher" with no verb at all. What Turkish asks of you instead — suffix order and harmony — is perfectly regular.

Try the system yourself: take any translation from the translator, find the verb at the end, then peel the suffixes off one by one. Our questions and pronouns guide gives you the other half of the toolkit. Turkish rewards this kind of detective work faster than almost any language.

What is the difference between sen and siz?

Both mean "you". Sen is friendly and singular — for friends, family and children. Siz is polite and is also the plural "you" — for strangers, elders and formal situations. When in doubt, use siz; our questions and pronouns guide explains the choice in full.
